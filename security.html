Programs in boxes, and you in charge

Every .deb, Flatpak, AppImage and plugin runs in a box that starts with nothing. A window like a phone's asks before anything is shared, and every switch starts off.

A box eachDebian programs, Flatpaks, AppImages, plugins and AI agents are fenced in
Off until you sayA new box has no network, sound, microphone or files
A dot when watchedThe dot is orange, teal or blue when the microphone, camera or screen is in use

The boxes

A box is a fence around a program, built with bubblewrap, the same tool Flatpak uses.

Each .deb apartIt goes into a folder of its own with its own libraries and runs in a box. Nothing is installed for the whole computer. When you remove the app, everything that belongs to it goes.
Starts closedA new box has no network, sound, microphone, older-style windows or files of yours. System folders are read-only. Nothing else is shared until you allow it.
Refused if it doesn't fitTrindle turns down a .deb that contains a driver or a background service, and explains why in plain words.
Kept patchedWhen Debian fixes a package that a box contains, a daily timer replaces the copy. This happens on mains power only.
Restricted by default, but not sealed off. Boxes still share the screen and the graphics card with everything else.

What a box keeps away from a program

Your filesThe box gets none of your files until you allow them. Allowing shows it only Documents, Downloads, Pictures, Music, Videos and Desktop. It never sees your whole home folder or your hidden settings files, so nothing in the box can make itself start outside it.
The screenA box can't take screenshots, read what you copy at any moment, fake a key press or draw over other windows, unless you allow the screen.
The microphonePlaying sound and listening are separate. A box that is allowed sound gets a small guard that lets playback through and refuses every request to record.
Older programsWhen a box needs X11, it gets its own Xwayland, never the desktop's, so it can't watch other apps.
Your computer's detailsA box sees only the few items in the hardware list that graphics need. It doesn't see the make, the serial number or the full parts list.
Risky requestsA short filter refuses things ordinary apps never need: typing into the terminal that started them, loading kernel parts, tracing other programs, changing the clock, and building a box inside the box.

What runs in a box

Debian programsEvery .deb you open runs in its own folder.
FlatpaksThey use bubblewrap themselves.
AppImages and pluginsEach runs in its own box. A plugin gets only the permissions it declared.
Programs started from FoldersWhen you use Open or Open with.
Downloaded filesA file in Downloads, or one marked by your browser, opens in a box with no network and none of your folders. The box holds only that file, read-only. A notice tells you, and you can switch this off in Settings, Permissions.
AI coding agentsIn a fence of their own, described below.
A limit, stated plainly. A program you start outside a box, from a shell or from your browser's own Open button, is not fenced. The first time one reaches the network or opens your files, Trindle tells you afterwards, once, but it can't stop it. Trindle's own parts and the system itself stay outside the boxes.

What a permission window shows

Plain words"Your Documents and Pictures folders", "take pictures of your screen", "your microphone". Each line says what goes wrong without it.
Off until you chooseEvery switch starts off. Allow all switches on everything that can be allowed, and every warning stays in view.
A warning when it looks oddIf a calculator asks for the internet, or a photo app asks for your keyboard, Trindle shows a plain warning. Risky permissions always carry one.
Who decidesYou do. A Flatpak or a plugin says what it wants. A .deb says nothing, so Trindle works out what it probably needs from what it is built with and its category, and tells you it is a guess.
Again after an updateIf an update wants more than before, the window comes back.
Change it laterIn Settings, System, Permissions there is one row per app, with a round icon for each permission. The icon is filled when the permission is allowed.

The window takes the keyboard as it opens, so it works without a mouse, and Escape leaves the choices as they were. The desktop passes no made-up key press or click to the window, so a program can't answer for you.

Permissions

When an app is installed, Trindle lists everything it wants in plain words, with a switch for each one. All the switches start off.

A box can ask for the internet, sound, the microphone, your folders, the camera and plugged-in devices, Bluetooth, notifications, to start by itself at login, the screen, older windows, or to work outside its box (meant for terminals). A plugin can also ask to share your mouse and keyboard with another computer, read the files you copy, or reach only your home network.

You always know who is watching or listening.

A dot shows in the top right corner while any app uses the camera or the microphone, or records the screen. It is orange for the microphone, teal for the camera and blue for the screen. It sits on the app's window tab too. One button in the ring cuts the microphone for every program, and only you can turn it back on.

AI coding agents in a box

Programs such as Claude Code, Codex, Cursor's agent, Gemini, Aider, OpenCode, Goose and Amp (you can edit the list) run in a fence of their own. They can read the system, the logs and the installed programs, and run commands, but they can't see your files.

They see an empty private home folder of their own, kept between runs, not yours. Other people's home folders, your mail, your keyring, ssh-agent and the session are hidden. Other programs are invisible to them, and anything that looks like a key or token is removed from their environment. They get no screen, sound or keyboard, so they can't see the screen or press keys.

Limited protection, not a guarantee. A determined program, a flaw in the computer's software, or you approving a request can let something through. The network is open by default, so an agent could send out anything it can read. An optional switch limits it to hosts you list.

You choose what the fence shares

An agent can ask for a folder, and a window outside the fence asks you: Share for this session only, Share always or No. Hidden folders such as .ssh and .gnupg, and mail and password folders, can never be shared.

Inside the fence, ssh and sudo are stand-ins that ask Trindle, outside the fence, to do the job. ssh works only with computers you list, using a key made for the fence. sudo works only after you approve the exact command in a window. We haven't tried those two on a real screen yet.

What it can read without asking

The desktopOpen windows, installed apps, running programs and their memory use.
The machineVersion, memory, battery, network, volume, look and disk space.
Settings and logsThe Trindle settings it is allowed to read, the latest lines of the system log and recent Trindle crashes.
DevicesSpeakers, microphones, Bluetooth, Wi-Fi in range and drives.

What it can do only after you say yes

An on-screen window asks first. The cross, Escape, closing the window, or two minutes of silence all count as no. After you say yes, it can start an app, bring a window to the front, close a window, change one Trindle setting from a fixed list, set light or dark and the accent colour, install or remove a Flathub app, change a Flathub app's permissions, join a known or open Wi-Fi network, pair a Bluetooth device, or undo a previous change.

An assistant, inside a fence

Trindle has a small MCP server, the standard way an AI assistant such as Claude talks to programs on your computer. It runs on your computer only and is not a network service.

Off until you sayIt does nothing until you tell your assistant, in the assistant's own settings, to start trindle-mcp. Trindle's Settings has no switch for it.
A snapshot firstBefore each change it saves Trindle's settings, every Flathub app's permissions and the list of apps. On a computer set up by the Trindle Installer, it takes a whole-system snapshot before it installs or removes an app too. Undo puts back settings and permissions only, not an app install.
Out of boundsIt can never touch the core, the compositor or the packaging system, switch snapshots off, or change its own rules. If a tool isn't on its built-in list, the assistant can't use it.
Not your filesNo tool opens your documents, photos or home folder. What it reads, such as window titles, is treated as data, and it must never obey it. It never sends Wi-Fi passwords.

Nothing needs an assistant. You can still do everything by hand, offline, with the mouse and keyboard.

Snapshots before and after

Trindle takes a snapshot before and after every package change, and the start-up menu lists the last twelve, so you can start from one even if the computer won't start. This needs the Btrfs disk the Trindle Installer makes.

The safety net

A daily copy of your files

The installer switches on a daily snapshot of your home folder. Each day's copy is kept for a week by default, or for three days, two weeks or a month if you choose. One switch turns it off. A computer upgraded from an older version may have it off to begin with.

A locked disk, or not

At the start, the installer asks whether to encrypt the disk. You can choose not at all (the starting choice), a password that you type at every start, or this computer's TPM chip, which needs a TPM 2 chip. Encryption needs a computer that starts with UEFI. There is no separate recovery key, so the password is the way back in.

The three choices

Updates, plugins, the network and the sockets

Security updatesDebian's security fixes install by themselves every day. This is on by default, and you can turn it off with the Security updates switch in Settings, About this computer. Debian's package system checks Debian's own signatures.
Plugins from a signed listPlugins come only from a catalogue that Trindle signs. Trindle checks the signature against the exact bytes of the list before it reads the list, and checks each plugin file against its SHA-256. You can't install a plugin from anywhere else.
A downloaded .debWhen you open one, Apps shows the site it came from and checks it against a public list of bad sites. The check happens on your computer. Apps also tells you the list isn't complete.
Ports only for what you allowTrindle can manage the firewall. A program that needs other computers to reach it names its ports, and Trindle opens only those ports, and only to home-network addresses. It closes them when you take the permission away.
Adverts and bad sites blockedOne switch blocks adverts, trackers and known bad sites in every browser and every app. Name lookups are private and encrypted. It runs on your own computer.
Wi-Fi that can't follow youEach Wi-Fi network sees a different made-up hardware address for your computer, so shops and stations can't follow it from place to place.
An unknown keyboardWhile the screen is locked, a new keyboard or mouse that you plug in does nothing until you unlock it.
Thunderbolt asks firstA Thunderbolt or USB4 device can reach the computer's memory directly, so Trindle tells you its name and asks whether to allow it always, just this time, or not at all.

Still being built

These are planned and are not in Trindle yet.

  • Locking the system files by default. You can already lock them with a switch in Settings, Snapshots, but the Trindle Installer doesn't turn it on yet.
  • The Permissions page listing the boxes round AppImages and programs started from Folders.
  • An assistant that can restart a misbehaving program, change that program's own settings or clear its cache, and never change Trindle itself.
  • A signed whole-system image, and a signed start-up for Trindle itself.
