Programs in boxes, and you in charge

Every .deb, Flatpak, AppImage and plugin runs in a box that starts with nothing. A window like a phone’s asks before anything is shared, and every switch starts off.

A box eachDebian programs, Flatpaks, AppImages, plugins and AI agents are fenced in
Off until you sayA new box has no network, sound, microphone or files
A dot when watchedOrange, teal or blue whenever the microphone, camera or screen is in use

The boxes

A box is a fence around a program, built with bubblewrap, the same tool Flatpak uses. A .deb you open goes into a folder of its own with its own libraries, and runs inside a box. There is no “install for the whole computer” choice. Remove the app and its folder and private home go with it, leaving nothing behind.

A new box has no network, no sound, no microphone, no older-style windows and none of your files. It sees the system folders read only, its own private home folder, and a screen connection of its own. Nothing else is given until you allow it.

A .deb that holds a driver or a background service is refused in plain words, because it cannot work in a box. Boxes are patched: when Debian fixes a package a box carries, a daily timer replaces the box’s copy, on mains power only.

Restricted by default, not sealed off. Boxes still share the screen and the graphics card with everything else.

What a box keeps from a program

Your filesNone until you allow them. Allowing shows only Documents, Downloads, Pictures, Music, Videos and Desktop, never the whole home folder and never hidden settings files, so nothing in the box can make itself start outside it.
The screenA box cannot take screenshots, read what you copy at any moment, fake a key press or draw over other windows, unless you allow the screen.
The microphonePlaying sound and listening are separate. A box allowed sound gets a small guard that passes playback and refuses every request to record.
Older programsWhen a box needs X11 it gets an Xwayland of its own, never the desktop’s, so it cannot watch other apps.
Your computer’s detailsA box sees only the few parts of the hardware list that graphics need, not the make, the serial number or the full parts list.
Risky requestsA short filter refuses what ordinary apps never need: typing into the terminal that started them, loading kernel parts, tracing other programs, changing the clock, building a box inside the box.

What runs in a box

Debian programsEvery .deb you open, in its own folder.
FlatpaksThey use bubblewrap themselves.
AppImages and pluginsEach in its own box, a plugin with only the permissions it declared.
Programs started from FoldersWith Open or Open with.
Downloaded filesA file in Downloads, or marked by your browser, opens in a box with no network and none of your folders, and only that file, read only. A notice says so, and you can switch it off in Settings, Permissions.
AI coding agentsIn a fence of their own, described below.
A limit, said plainly. A program you start outside a box, from a shell or from your browser’s own Open button, is not fenced. Trindle tells you afterwards, once, when one first reaches the network or has your files open, but it cannot stop it. Trindle’s own parts and the system itself stay outside the boxes.

What a permission window shows

Plain words“Your Documents and Pictures folders”, “take pictures of your screen”, “your microphone”. Each line says what goes wrong without it.
Off until you chooseEvery switch starts off. Allow all switches on everything that can be allowed, and every warning stays in view.
A warning when it looks oddA calculator asking for the internet, or a photo app asking for your keyboard, comes with a plain warning. Risky permissions always carry one.
Who decidesYou do. A Flatpak or a plugin says what it wants. A .deb says nothing, so Trindle works out what it probably needs from what it is built with and its category, and says so.
Again after an updateIf an update wants more than before, the window comes back.
Change it laterSettings, System, Permissions has one row per app, with a round icon for each permission, filled when allowed.

The window takes the keyboard as it opens, so it works without a mouse, and Escape leaves the choices as they were. The desktop passes no made-up key press or click to it, so a program cannot answer for you.

Permissions

When an app is installed, Trindle lists everything it wants in plain words, with a switch for each, all off until you choose.

A box can ask for the internet, sound, the microphone, your folders, the camera and plugged-in devices, Bluetooth, notifications, to start by itself at login, the screen, older windows, or to work outside its box (meant for terminals). A plugin can also ask to share your mouse and keyboard with another computer, read the files you copy, or reach your home network only.

You always know who is watching or listening.

A dot shows in the top right corner while any app uses the camera, the microphone or records the screen: orange for the microphone, teal for the camera, blue for the screen. It sits on the app’s window tab too. One button in the ring cuts the microphone for every program, and only your own press turns it back on.

AI coding agents in a box

Programs such as Claude Code, Codex, Cursor’s agent, Gemini, Aider, OpenCode, Goose and Amp (the list can be edited) run in a fence of their own. They can read the system, the logs and the installed programs, and run commands, but they cannot see your files.

They see an empty private home folder, kept between runs, not yours. Other people’s homes, mail, your keyring, ssh-agent and the session are hidden, other programs are invisible to them, and anything that looks like a key or token is cleaned out of their environment. They get no screen, sound or keyboard, so they cannot see the screen or press keys.

Limited protection, not a guarantee. A determined program, a flaw in the computer’s software, or you approving a request can let something through. The network is open by default, so anything an agent can read it could send out; an optional switch limits it to hosts you list.

You choose what the fence shares

An agent can ask for a folder, and a window outside the fence asks you: Share for this session only, Share always or No. Hidden folders such as .ssh and .gnupg, and mail and password folders, can never be shared.

Inside the fence, ssh and sudo are stand-ins that ask Trindle outside. ssh works only to computers you list, with a key made for the fence, and sudo only after you approve the exact command in a window. Those two have not yet been tried on a real screen.

What it can read without asking

The desktopOpen windows, installed apps, running programs and their memory use.
The machineVersion, memory, battery, network, volume, look and disk space.
Settings and logsThe Trindle settings it may read, the latest system log lines and recent Trindle crashes.
DevicesSpeakers, microphones, Bluetooth, Wi-Fi in range and drives.

What it can do only after you say yes

An on-screen window asks first. The cross, Escape, closing it or two minutes of silence all mean no. It can then start an app, bring a window to the front, close a window, change one Trindle setting from a fixed list, set light or dark and the accent colour, install or remove a Flathub app, change a Flathub app’s permissions, join a known or open Wi-Fi network, pair a Bluetooth device, or undo a previous change.

An assistant, inside a fence

Trindle has a small MCP server, the standard way an AI assistant such as Claude talks to programs on your computer. It runs on your computer only and is not a network service.

It is built in and does nothing until an assistant is pointed at it: your assistant has to be told, in its own settings, to start trindle-mcp. Leave that out and it is never connected. Settings has no switch for it.

Before each change it takes a snapshot of Trindle’s settings, every Flathub app’s permissions and the list of apps, and before installing or removing an app it takes a whole-system snapshot too. It can never touch the core, the compositor or the packaging system, switch snapshots off, or change its own rules. The list of things it can do is built in, so a tool not on it does not exist.

It reads the live computer, not your personal files: no tool opens documents, photos or your home folder. Everything it reads, such as window titles and log lines, is passed on as data it must never obey, so text planted by a stranger cannot pass for an instruction. It never sends Wi-Fi passwords: you type them yourself.

Nothing needs an assistant. Everything can still be done by hand, offline, with the mouse and keyboard.

Snapshots before and after

A snapshot is taken before and after every software change, and the start-up menu lists the last twelve, so you can start from one even if the computer will not start. This needs the Btrfs disk the Trindle Installer makes.

The safety net

A daily copy of your files

A snapshot of your home folder is taken each day and kept for a week by default, or three days, two weeks or a month. One switch turns it off. Opening an older copy from Folders is still being built.

A locked disk, or not

The installer asks first whether to encrypt the disk: not at all (the starting choice), with a password typed at every start, or with this computer’s TPM chip, which needs a TPM 2 chip. Encryption needs a computer that starts with UEFI. There is no separate recovery key: the password is the way back in.

The three choices

Updates, plugins, the network and the sockets

Security updatesDebian’s security fixes install by themselves every day. They are on by default, and you can turn them off with the Security updates switch in Settings. Debian’s package system checks Debian’s own signatures.
Plugins from a signed listPlugins come only from a catalogue Trindle signs. The signature is checked over the exact bytes before the list is read, and each plugin file is checked against its SHA-256. There is no way to install a plugin from anywhere else.
A downloaded .debWhen you open one, Apps shows the site it came from and checks it against a public list of bad sites, on your computer. It says the list is not complete.
Ports only for what you allowTrindle can manage the firewall. A program that needs other computers to reach it names its ports, and Trindle opens only those, to home-network addresses only, and closes them when you take the permission away.
Adverts and bad sites blockedOne switch blocks adverts, trackers and known bad sites for every browser and every app, with private, encrypted name lookups. It runs on your own computer.
A Wi-Fi that cannot follow youEach Wi-Fi network sees a made-up hardware address of its own for your computer, so shops and stations cannot follow it between places.
A strange keyboardWhile the screen is locked, a new keyboard or mouse plugged in does nothing until you unlock.
Thunderbolt asks firstA Thunderbolt or USB4 device can reach the computer’s memory directly, so Trindle names it and asks: allow it always, just this time, or not at all.

Still being built

These are planned and are not in Trindle yet.

  • Locking the system files. The way to lock them exists, but the installer does not turn it on yet.
  • Opening yesterday’s version of a file from Folders.
  • The Permissions page listing the boxes round AppImages and programs started from Folders.
  • An assistant that can restart a misbehaving program, change that program’s own settings or clear its cache, and never change Trindle itself.
  • A signed whole-system image, and a signed start-up for Trindle itself.

No bar, no dock, no menu. A desktop with just one ring, written in Rust and made for a mouse or a touchscreen.
